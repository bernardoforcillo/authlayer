authlayer has **five** persistence ports, and they are independent. You can
implement one and not the others, and the two shipped backends implement all
five.

| Port | Holds | Methods |
|---|---|---|
| `scope.Store[C, M]` | containers, members, custom roles, a transaction | composed from `ContainerStore`, `MemberStore`, `RoleStore` |
| `invite.Store` | email invitations and links | 13 |
| `apikey.Store` | [service accounts](/service-accounts/overview) and their keys | 14 |
| `auth.Store` | users, sessions, verifications | 18 |
| `auth.IdentityStore` | [external identities](/oauth/overview) — **optional** | 6 |

<Note>
`auth.IdentityStore` is a port of its own rather than six more methods on
`auth.Store`, because `auth.Store` is released: adding to it would break every
third-party backend that already implements it. An application offering no
external sign-in wires none, creates no table, and gets `ErrOAuthNotConfigured`
from the four methods that would need it.
</Note>

## A store is pure persistence

<Note>
The engine stamps ids, owners and timestamps, resolves roles, and runs **every**
authorization check before calling in. The store never authorizes anything and
never interprets permission bytes.
</Note>

What it *does* owe the engine is the right **sentinel error** when a lookup
finds nothing — `ErrNotMember` rather than a generic not-found — because the
engine branches on those. Each method's contract is documented on the
interface; `go doc ./scope Store` is the reference.

`scope.Store` is composed from three smaller interfaces, so a backend or a
decorator — caching, metrics, multi-tenant routing — can override a slice of it
by embedding the rest.

<Tip>
If you need fewer round trips, wrap the **`Store`**, not the `Service`. A
decorator over the port keeps every authorization rule intact while changing
where the bytes come from. authlayer caches nothing itself, on purpose: a
permission change takes effect immediately and there is no invalidation to get
wrong.
</Tip>

## `WithTx` is only used in one place

`scope.Store.WithTx(ctx, fn)` runs `fn` inside a single transaction, passing a
tx-scoped `Store` that `fn` must use for its writes. The engine uses it for
`CreateContainer`, where the container, the owner's membership and the
`ContainerCreated` hook must commit together or not at all.

A backend without real transactions may approximate it — `store/memory`
snapshots and restores under a mutex — but a production backend should use the
database's.

<Warning>
There is **no cross-store transaction anywhere**. Invitation acceptance spans
`invite.Store` and `scope.Store`, which may not even share a database — and so
does a [service account](/service-accounts/overview#what-is-not-atomic), whose
record and membership live one in each — and `auth`, `scope`, `invite` and
`apikey` declare no foreign keys between their tables. That
constraint is what shapes
[claim-first-admit-second](/invitations/security#claim-first-admit-second), what
leaves the
[verification sweeps sequential-only](/authentication/passwords#every-sweep-is-sequential-only),
and what leaves one window in external sign-in
[open and documented](/oauth/sign-in) rather than closed.
</Warning>

## The strictest port

`auth.Store` carries **eleven MUSTs across its twenty-two methods**, and they
are not all the same kind of obligation. Four of them arrived with
[account deletion](/authentication/account-deletion), which grew the port from
the eighteen methods it shipped with.

<AccordionGroup>

<Accordion title="Five demand atomicity" icon="lock">
`MarkRotated`, `CreateSuccessorSession`, `MarkEmailVerified`, `UpdateUserEmail`,
`MarkUserDeleted`.

Splitting any of them into a read and a later write **reopens a security hole**
rather than merely narrowing a race: two successful rotations of one token; a
successor resurrecting a family that was revoked mid-rotation; an address
certified that nobody proved control of; two accounts sharing one address.

`MarkUserDeleted` is the one whose two halves fail in **opposite** directions,
which is why its five field writes have to land together. *Stamped but not
scrubbed*: every entry point already refuses the row while it still holds the
real address and the live password hash, so the user was told the account was
anonymized and it is not. *Scrubbed but not stamped*: the row reports as a
**live** account whose address is derived from the user id — not a secret — and
a caller who guesses it can drive a password reset against an account that was
supposed to be closed.

That last one has nothing above it to catch it — `RequestEmailChange`
[deliberately does not pre-check the new address](/authentication/email-verification#the-uniqueness-check-happens-at-redemption-not-before),
so `UpdateUserEmail` at redemption is the only enforcement point there is.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Two are what SignUp's enumeration safety leans on" icon="eye-slash">
`CreateUser` must decide `ErrEmailTaken` from the **same attempt that performs
the write**, and `FindUserByEmail` must **read its own writes**.

Either one violated turns a single `SignUp` call into an "is this address
registered?" oracle from inside the store, where no amount of care in `SignUp`
itself can see it.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Two demand the opposite: an extra read, and serialization" icon="arrows-rotate">
`DeleteSessionsByFamily` carries two MUSTs that apply to any backend whose
`CreateSuccessorSession` holds a row-level lock on the predecessor for a
transaction's duration — the shape `store/drops` uses.

A single autocommit `DELETE FROM sessions WHERE family_id = $1` is **not
sufficient** there: its snapshot is taken *before* it waits for that lock, so
once unblocked it deletes only what existed at the earlier instant and misses
the successor committed while it waited — leaving a revoked family with one
live, fully rotating session.

Such a backend must re-snapshot **after** the wait (a `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE`
over the family's rows, then the `DELETE`, in one transaction), and must
**additionally serialize** concurrent calls on the same family, because that
locking `SELECT` has no ordering guarantee and two callers can deadlock each
other. A per-family advisory transaction lock taken before the `SELECT` is
sufficient; an `ORDER BY` on the `SELECT` is **not**, since the `DELETE` that
follows has no ordering of its own.

A backend whose `CreateSuccessorSession` takes no such lock —
`store/memory`, whose single mutex spans both methods' whole bodies — has no gap
to close and owes neither.

`DeleteSessionsByUser` inherits both MUSTs unchanged, because a whole-user
revocation is a superset of a family one. The survivor it would otherwise leave
is a fully rotating refresh token for an account whose owner has just asked for
it to be deleted — the worst possible moment for one to outlive its sweep.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Two constrain the shape of the deletion cascade" icon="broom">
Both are about what a backend must **not** quietly do for you.

`DeleteUser` removes the user row **only**, and must not cascade to that
user's sessions or verifications. A backend whose
`FOREIGN KEY ... ON DELETE CASCADE` performs the sweep as a side effect has
taken the [ordering decision](/authentication/account-deletion#the-order-is-fail-safe-and-is-not-negotiable)
away from the caller and made it unobservable: a caller that skipped the sweeps
entirely and one that ran them in the right order look identical afterwards,
against that backend and no other.

`DeleteVerificationsByUser` must filter on `userID` **alone**, never on a list
of purposes it knows about. `Purpose` is an open string this port neither
validates nor enumerates, so implementing this as a fan-out over
`DeleteVerificationsByUserAndPurpose` does not satisfy it — no such list can be
complete, and a purpose added by a later flow, or minted by a deployment
itself, would walk straight through the sweep.

The two by-user sweeps both answer a match of zero rows with `nil`; `DeleteUser`
answers `ErrUserNotFound`, because a caller deleting an account needs to know
whether there was one.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Two more sit on the record types, not on a method" icon="fingerprint">
`Session.TokenHash` and `Verification.TokenHash` each carry a **uniqueness**
MUST — a `UNIQUE` constraint in a SQL backend.

`MarkRotated`'s single-winner contract breaks without it **with no atomicity
defect at all**: two rows sharing a hash lets two concurrent callers each
correctly, atomically win the row they happened to pick. A backend that
satisfies every method obligation above and skips these is still wrong.
</Accordion>

</AccordionGroup>

## The eight invitation MUSTs

All about the same thing: bounding **who gets in**.

**Four are on methods.** `ConsumeLink` must make its check and its increment one
atomic step, or a `MaxUses: 1` link admits everyone who clicks it at once.
`DeleteEmailInvite` must be **rows-affected gated**, because that gate — not any
check above it — is what makes one emailed token pay out once; `AcceptInvite`
claims first and grants second precisely so it is the only thing that has to
hold. And `CreateEmailInvite` and `CreateLink` must refuse a write that would
break a uniqueness constraint, **atomically with performing it**.

**Four are on the record types**, because they constrain the shape of the table
rather than the behaviour of one call:

<Columns cols={2}>
  <Card title="EmailInvite.TokenHash" icon="fingerprint">
    Unique. Two rows sharing a hash defeats the single-winner property with no
    atomicity defect at all.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Link.Code" icon="link">
    Unique. Two concurrent redeemers resolve **different** rows and each wins
    the one it picked — the limit bounds nothing while every individual consume
    behaves perfectly.
  </Card>
  <Card title="(ContainerID, Email)" icon="table-columns">
    Unique **as a pair**. What makes re-inviting an address replace rather than
    duplicate when two calls race. The same person invited to two containers is
    two legitimate rows.
  </Card>
  <Card title="EmailInvite.Email is normalized" icon="wand-magic-sparkles">
    `invite.NormalizeEmail` on every address a store writes or matches — the
    write in `CreateEmailInvite`, the argument to `DeleteEmailInvitesFor`.
    Without it the pair above is byte-exact, so two casings of one address are
    two live tokens for one person.
  </Card>
</Columns>

## The three identity MUSTs

`auth.IdentityStore` is small, and all three of its **MUSTs** bound one thing:
who can sign in as whom.

<Columns cols={2}>
  <Card title="(Provider, Subject) is unique" icon="fingerprint">
    A composite `UNIQUE` in a SQL backend. Without it two rows can name one
    external account against two **different** local users, and a sign-in
    resolving that subject lands on whichever row the backend returns first —
    decided by row order.
  </Card>
  <Card title="CreateIdentity decides and writes in one step" icon="lock">
    `ErrIdentityLinked` must come from the write attempt itself, never from a
    separate read that a concurrent link could race. An existing row is never
    silently replaced or re-pointed at another user.
  </Card>
  <Card title="DeleteIdentityIfNotLast is atomic" icon="shield-halved">
    The reachability check and the delete are **one** step: one statement, one
    transaction, or one acquisition of the mutex. A read-then-write there
    [locks a user out permanently](/oauth/connected-accounts), silently, with
    both callers told they succeeded.
  </Card>
</Columns>

<Note>
`DeleteIdentity` — the sixth method — is the by-id sibling and makes **no**
reachability check at all: it is how the service retracts a row it wrote itself
(a link whose account moved out from under it) and how `ResetPassword` sweeps
identities after it has already committed a new password. Never reach for it
from a connected-accounts screen; that is `DeleteIdentityIfNotLast`'s job.
</Note>

<Warning>
A fourth obligation is not a MUST the port can state, because it is about what a
backend must **not** add: an identity row holds `(provider, subject, email)` and
two timestamps, and [no provider token of any kind](/oauth/overview). A backend
that "helpfully" stores the access token it was never given has moved the
library's boundary on its users' behalf.
</Warning>

## They are executable, not just prose

The two **strict** ports ship their contract as a runnable suite, so a
third-party backend author can check their work. `auth.IdentityStore` does not
have one — its obligations are covered by `store/memory`'s and `store/drops`'
own tests, including a live concurrency test for the atomicity MUST above:

{/* verify:skip test-function bodies needing a *testing.T; both are compiled and run by the project's own suite — store/memory runs each in store/memory/auth_test.go and invite_test.go, and store/drops runs each in its integration lane */}
```go
authtest.RunStoreContract(t, newStore)   // 52 checks, 6 of them races
invitetest.RunStoreContract(t, newStore) // 46 checks, 8 of them races
```
