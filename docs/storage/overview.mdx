authlayer has **three** persistence ports, and they are independent. You can
implement one and not the others, and the two shipped backends implement all
three.

| Port | Holds | Methods |
|---|---|---|
| `scope.Store[C, M]` | containers, members, custom roles, a transaction | composed from `ContainerStore`, `MemberStore`, `RoleStore` |
| `invite.Store` | email invitations and links | 13 |
| `auth.Store` | users, sessions, verifications | 18 |

## A store is pure persistence

<Note>
The engine stamps ids, owners and timestamps, resolves roles, and runs **every**
authorization check before calling in. The store never authorizes anything and
never interprets permission bytes.
</Note>

What it *does* owe the engine is the right **sentinel error** when a lookup
finds nothing — `ErrNotMember` rather than a generic not-found — because the
engine branches on those. Each method's contract is documented on the
interface; `go doc ./scope Store` is the reference.

`scope.Store` is composed from three smaller interfaces, so a backend or a
decorator — caching, metrics, multi-tenant routing — can override a slice of it
by embedding the rest.

<Tip>
If you need fewer round trips, wrap the **`Store`**, not the `Service`. A
decorator over the port keeps every authorization rule intact while changing
where the bytes come from. authlayer caches nothing itself, on purpose: a
permission change takes effect immediately and there is no invalidation to get
wrong.
</Tip>

## `WithTx` is only used in one place

`scope.Store.WithTx(ctx, fn)` runs `fn` inside a single transaction, passing a
tx-scoped `Store` that `fn` must use for its writes. The engine uses it for
`CreateContainer`, where the container, the owner's membership and the
`ContainerCreated` hook must commit together or not at all.

A backend without real transactions may approximate it — `store/memory`
snapshots and restores under a mutex — but a production backend should use the
database's.

<Warning>
There is **no cross-store transaction anywhere**. Invitation acceptance spans
`invite.Store` and `scope.Store`, which may not even share a database, and
`auth`, `scope` and `invite` declare no foreign keys between their tables. That
constraint is what shapes
[claim-first-admit-second](/invitations/security#claim-first-admit-second) and
what leaves the
[verification sweeps sequential-only](/authentication/passwords#both-sweeps-are-sequential-only).
</Warning>

## The strictest port

`auth.Store` carries **seven MUSTs across its eighteen methods**, and they are
not all the same kind of obligation.

<AccordionGroup>

<Accordion title="Four demand atomicity" icon="lock">
`MarkRotated`, `CreateSuccessorSession`, `MarkEmailVerified`, `UpdateUserEmail`.

Splitting any of them into a read and a later write **reopens a security hole**
rather than merely narrowing a race: two successful rotations of one token; a
successor resurrecting a family that was revoked mid-rotation; an address
certified that nobody proved control of; two accounts sharing one address.

That last one has nothing above it to catch it — `RequestEmailChange`
[deliberately does not pre-check the new address](/authentication/email-verification#the-uniqueness-check-happens-at-redemption-not-before),
so `UpdateUserEmail` at redemption is the only enforcement point there is.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Two are what SignUp's enumeration safety leans on" icon="eye-slash">
`CreateUser` must decide `ErrEmailTaken` from the **same attempt that performs
the write**, and `FindUserByEmail` must **read its own writes**.

Either one violated turns a single `SignUp` call into an "is this address
registered?" oracle from inside the store, where no amount of care in `SignUp`
itself can see it.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="One demands the opposite: an extra read, and serialization" icon="arrows-rotate">
`DeleteSessionsByFamily` carries two MUSTs that apply to any backend whose
`CreateSuccessorSession` holds a row-level lock on the predecessor for a
transaction's duration — the shape `store/drops` uses.

A single autocommit `DELETE FROM sessions WHERE family_id = $1` is **not
sufficient** there: its snapshot is taken *before* it waits for that lock, so
once unblocked it deletes only what existed at the earlier instant and misses
the successor committed while it waited — leaving a revoked family with one
live, fully rotating session.

Such a backend must re-snapshot **after** the wait (a `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE`
over the family's rows, then the `DELETE`, in one transaction), and must
**additionally serialize** concurrent calls on the same family, because that
locking `SELECT` has no ordering guarantee and two callers can deadlock each
other. A per-family advisory transaction lock taken before the `SELECT` is
sufficient; an `ORDER BY` on the `SELECT` is **not**, since the `DELETE` that
follows has no ordering of its own.

A backend whose `CreateSuccessorSession` takes no such lock —
`store/memory`, whose single mutex spans both methods' whole bodies — has no gap
to close and owes neither.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Two more sit on the record types, not on a method" icon="fingerprint">
`Session.TokenHash` and `Verification.TokenHash` each carry a **uniqueness**
MUST — a `UNIQUE` constraint in a SQL backend.

`MarkRotated`'s single-winner contract breaks without it **with no atomicity
defect at all**: two rows sharing a hash lets two concurrent callers each
correctly, atomically win the row they happened to pick. A backend that
satisfies every method obligation above and skips these is still wrong.
</Accordion>

</AccordionGroup>

## The seven invitation MUSTs

All about the same thing: bounding **who gets in**.

**Four are on methods.** `ConsumeLink` must make its check and its increment one
atomic step, or a `MaxUses: 1` link admits everyone who clicks it at once.
`DeleteEmailInvite` must be **rows-affected gated**, because that gate — not any
check above it — is what makes one emailed token pay out once; `AcceptInvite`
claims first and grants second precisely so it is the only thing that has to
hold. And `CreateEmailInvite` and `CreateLink` must refuse a write that would
break a uniqueness constraint, **atomically with performing it**.

**Three are on the record types**, because they constrain the shape of the table
rather than the behaviour of one call:

<Columns cols={2}>
  <Card title="EmailInvite.TokenHash" icon="fingerprint">
    Unique. Two rows sharing a hash defeats the single-winner property with no
    atomicity defect at all.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Link.Code" icon="link">
    Unique. Two concurrent redeemers resolve **different** rows and each wins
    the one it picked — the limit bounds nothing while every individual consume
    behaves perfectly.
  </Card>
  <Card title="(ContainerID, Email)" icon="table-columns">
    Unique **as a pair**. What makes re-inviting an address replace rather than
    duplicate when two calls race. The same person invited to two containers is
    two legitimate rows.
  </Card>
</Columns>

## They are executable, not just prose

Both strict ports ship their contract as a runnable suite, so a third-party
backend author can check their work:

{/* verify:skip test-function bodies needing a *testing.T; both are compiled and run by the project's own suite — store/memory runs each in store/memory/auth_test.go and invite_test.go, and store/drops runs each in its integration lane */}
```go
authtest.RunStoreContract(t, newStore)   // 52 checks, 6 of them races
invitetest.RunStoreContract(t, newStore) // 43 checks, 8 of them races
```

See [Writing your own backend](/storage/custom-backends).
